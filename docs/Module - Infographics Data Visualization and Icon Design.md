## PART 1: WHAT ARE INFOGRAPHICS? (20 minutes)

### 1.1 Definition

An **infographic** is a visual representation of information, data, or knowledge designed to present complex content quickly and clearly. The term combines *information* and *graphic* — it is, at its core, the application of graphic design principles to the communication of facts, statistics, processes, and relationships.

*Mrantinkovic et al. (2020)* define graphic design as the practice of visual communication — the use of visual elements to convey a message to a specific audience. Infographics are one of the most direct expressions of this definition: they exist entirely to make information more accessible, more memorable, and more persuasive than plain text or raw data alone.

> **Key Concept:** An infographic is not decoration added to data. It is a translation — the designer's job is to find the visual form that most accurately and efficiently communicates the meaning hidden inside the numbers, steps, or relationships.

---

### 1.2 Why Infographics Work

The human brain processes visual information approximately 60,000 times faster than text. Patterns, comparisons, proportions, and hierarchies that would take paragraphs to describe in writing can be understood in seconds when expressed visually.

*Mrantinkovic et al. (2020)* identify **color**, **shape**, **line**, and **typography** as the fundamental elements of graphic design. In infographics, these elements do not merely decorate — they carry meaning:

| Design Element | Role in Infographics |
|---|---|
| **Color** | Encodes categories, signals importance, shows magnitude, creates hierarchy |
| **Shape** | Represents quantities (bar, pie, area), categories (icons), and relationships (arrows, connectors) |
| **Line** | Guides the eye, shows sequence, connects related elements, defines structure |
| **Typography** | Labels data points, provides context, establishes hierarchy between title, subtitle, and annotation |
| **Space** | Separates unrelated elements, groups related ones, prevents visual overload |

---

### 1.3 The Six Types of Infographics

Different types of data and messages require different visual structures. *Mrantinkovic et al. (2020)* emphasize that design decisions must always serve the communication goal — choosing the wrong infographic type distorts the message even when the data is accurate.

| Type | Best For | Key Visual Structure |
|---|---|---|
| **Statistical** | Presenting numerical data, comparisons, percentages, trends | Charts, graphs, pictographs, data callouts |
| **Informational** | Explaining a concept, defining terms, summarizing a topic | Text blocks, icons, dividers, hierarchy of headings |
| **Timeline** | Showing chronological events, history, project phases | Horizontal or vertical time axis with event markers |
| **Process / How-To** | Explaining steps, workflows, instructions, cycles | Numbered steps, flowcharts, arrows, decision trees |
| **Geographic** | Showing location-based data, regional comparisons, distribution | Maps with overlaid data (choropleth, dot, bubble maps) |
| **Comparison** | Contrasting two or more options, products, or approaches | Side-by-side columns, versus layouts, pros/cons tables |

> **Design Note:** Most real-world infographics combine two or more types. A statistical infographic about global coffee consumption might include a geographic map, a timeline of growth, and a comparison of top-producing countries — all within a single layout. The key is that each section uses the type most appropriate for its specific data.

---

### 1.4 Infographics vs. Data Visualization

These terms are often used interchangeably but have a meaningful distinction:

| | Infographic | Data Visualization |
|---|---|---|
| **Scope** | Combines data, text, icons, and narrative into a complete designed artifact | Focuses specifically on the visual encoding of data |
| **Audience** | General public — designed to be immediately accessible | Often technical — may require domain knowledge to interpret |
| **Design role** | High — layout, illustration, color, and typography are central | Moderate — clarity and accuracy take priority over aesthetics |
| **Examples** | Magazine feature, social media post, printed poster | Dashboard chart, scientific graph, interactive data tool |

> **Key Concept:** All infographics contain data visualization, but not all data visualization is an infographic. A bar chart in a spreadsheet is data visualization. That same bar chart redesigned with context, narrative, icons, and brand styling — published as a shareable visual — is an infographic.

## PART 2: PRINCIPLES OF DATA VISUALIZATION (25 minutes)

### 2.1 The Four Core Principles

Effective data visualization is governed by four principles that apply regardless of the chart type or design style *(Mrantinkovic et al., 2020)*:

**1. Accuracy**
The visual representation must be mathematically honest. A bar chart where the bars do not start at zero, a pie chart whose segments do not add up to 100%, or a scale that has been manipulated to exaggerate a trend — these are not design choices. They are misrepresentations of data.

> **Critical Rule:** Never distort the visual encoding of data to make a point look stronger than the numbers support. The designer's job is to reveal the truth in data, not to manufacture it.

**2. Hierarchy**
Not all data points are equally important. The most important insight — the headline finding — must be the most visually prominent element. Supporting data, context, and labels should be progressively smaller, lighter, or less saturated.

**3. Simplicity**
Every element in a data visualization must earn its place. Decorative elements that do not encode data — unnecessary gridlines, redundant labels, 3D effects, background textures — are called **chartjunk** (a term coined by statistician Edward Tufte). Chartjunk increases visual complexity without increasing information.

**4. Context**
Raw numbers without context are meaningless. "Sales increased by 12%" means nothing without knowing: compared to what period? Against what target? In what market conditions? The designer's job is to provide enough context for the audience to correctly interpret the data.

---

### 2.2 Choosing the Right Chart Type

The most common data visualization error is using the wrong chart type for the data. Each chart type is designed to show a specific kind of relationship.

| Relationship | Best Chart Type | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| **Comparison** (how values differ) | Bar chart, column chart | Pie chart (hard to compare slices) |
| **Composition** (parts of a whole) | Pie chart, donut chart, stacked bar | Line chart (implies change over time) |
| **Trend over time** | Line chart, area chart | Bar chart (works but less clear for trends) |
| **Distribution** (spread of values) | Histogram, box plot, dot plot | Pie chart (cannot show distribution) |
| **Relationship** (correlation between variables) | Scatter plot, bubble chart | Bar chart (cannot show two-variable correlation) |
| **Geographic** (location-based data) | Choropleth map, dot map | Bar chart (loses geographic context) |

> **Design Application:** Before choosing a chart type, ask: *What is the one thing I want the audience to understand from this data?* The answer determines the chart type. If the answer is "how these five values compare," use a bar chart. If the answer is "how this value has changed over 10 years," use a line chart.

---

### 2.3 Color in Data Visualization

*Mrantinkovic et al. (2020)* identify color as one of the most powerful elements of graphic design. In data visualization, color serves three specific functions:

**1. Categorical** — Different colors represent different categories (e.g., each country in a comparison gets a distinct color). Use colors that are clearly distinguishable from each other. Limit to 5–7 categories maximum — beyond that, colors become difficult to distinguish.

**2. Sequential** — A single color in a range of lightness values represents a range of quantities (e.g., light blue = low, dark blue = high). Used in heatmaps and choropleth maps. The audience intuitively reads darker as "more."

**3. Diverging** — Two contrasting colors meet at a neutral midpoint, representing values that diverge in two directions (e.g., red = below target, white = at target, green = above target). Used when the midpoint has meaningful significance.

**Color Rules for Data Visualization:**
- Use **one accent color** to highlight the most important data point — everything else should be neutral (grey)
- Never use color as the only way to distinguish data — always pair color with labels, patterns, or position (accessibility for color-blind users)
- Avoid rainbow color scales — they imply equal importance across all values and are difficult to interpret accurately
- Test your color palette in greyscale — if the data is still readable, the palette is robust

---

### 2.4 Typography in Data Visualization

Typography in infographics serves a strict hierarchy. *Mrantinkovic et al. (2020)* describe typography as a fundamental design element that communicates through both content and visual form.

| Level | Role | Recommended Style |
|---|---|---|
| **Title** | States the main insight or finding — the "headline" | Large, bold, high contrast — the first thing the eye lands on |
| **Subtitle / Annotation** | Provides context, explains the data source, or highlights a key finding | Medium weight, slightly smaller, muted color |
| **Axis labels / Category labels** | Identifies what each data point represents | Small, regular weight, consistent alignment |
| **Data labels** | Shows the exact value of a data point | Small, placed close to the data point, never overlapping |
| **Source / Caption** | Credits the data source — essential for credibility | Smallest text, bottom of the infographic, muted color |

> **Key Rule:** In data visualization, typography should be invisible — it should label and explain without drawing attention to itself. If the audience is noticing the font choices, the typography is too decorative.

---

### 2.5 Layout and Visual Flow

*Mrantinkovic et al. (2020)* describe **visual hierarchy** as the arrangement of elements to guide the viewer's eye in a deliberate sequence. In infographics, this sequence must follow the logic of the data story:

**The Inverted Pyramid Structure:**
1. **Headline insight** — the single most important finding, stated boldly at the top
2. **Supporting data** — the charts, graphs, and statistics that prove the headline
3. **Context and detail** — annotations, comparisons, and secondary data points
4. **Source and methodology** — data attribution at the bottom

**Grid-Based Layout:**
Infographics use a grid to align elements consistently. A simple 3-column or 4-column grid provides enough flexibility for varied content while maintaining visual order. All charts, text blocks, and icons should align to the grid — random placement creates visual noise that competes with the data.

## PART 3: ICON DESIGN (30 minutes)

### 3.1 What Is an Icon?

An **icon** is a small graphic symbol that represents an object, action, concept, or category. Icons are the visual vocabulary of interfaces, infographics, signage, and communication systems. They reduce complex ideas to their simplest recognizable form.

*Mrantinkovic et al. (2020)* describe graphic design as the use of visual elements to communicate to a specific audience. Icons are the most compressed form of this communication — a single shape that must be immediately understood without text support.

Icons appear in:
- User interfaces (apps, websites, operating systems)
- Infographics (replacing or supplementing text labels)
- Wayfinding and signage (airports, hospitals, public spaces)
- Presentations and reports (visual bullet points)
- Packaging and product labeling

---

### 3.2 The Three Levels of Icon Abstraction

Icons exist on a spectrum from literal to abstract:

| Level | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Realistic (Skeuomorphic)** | Closely resembles the real-world object — detailed, textured, three-dimensional | A photo-realistic camera icon with lens reflections |
| **Simplified (Flat)** | Retains the recognizable form of the object but removes all detail and texture | A flat camera outline with a simple circle for the lens |
| **Abstract (Symbolic)** | No longer resembles the object — represents the concept through convention or metaphor | A gear icon for "settings" (gears don't look like software settings) |

> **Design Note:** For most modern applications — interfaces, infographics, and signage — **simplified flat icons** are the professional standard. They are legible at small sizes, work in any color, and age well. Realistic icons are difficult to maintain at small sizes and feel dated quickly. Abstract icons require established conventions to be understood.

---

### 3.3 Principles of Effective Icon Design

**1. Clarity at Small Sizes**
An icon must be immediately recognizable at its smallest intended display size — typically 16×16 px for interface icons and 24×24 px for infographic icons. If the concept cannot be communicated clearly at that size, the design is too complex.

**2. Consistency Within a System**
Icons are almost never designed in isolation — they are part of a **system**. Every icon in a set must share:
- The same stroke weight (line thickness)
- The same corner radius (sharp vs. rounded)
- The same level of detail and abstraction
- The same visual weight (how "heavy" or "light" the icon feels)
- The same grid and padding

An icon set where some icons are outlined and others are filled, or where stroke weights vary randomly, looks unprofessional and creates visual noise.

**3. Universality**
The best icons are understood across cultures and languages. A house icon for "home," an envelope for "email," a magnifying glass for "search" — these have become universal conventions. When designing new icons, lean on established conventions before inventing new ones. Originality in icon design is a liability, not an asset.

**4. Simplicity**
*Mrantinkovic et al. (2020)* identify simplicity as a core quality of effective graphic design. In icon design, this means: use the minimum number of shapes and lines needed to make the concept recognizable. Every additional detail reduces legibility at small sizes.

---

### 3.4 Geometric Construction of Icons

Professional icons are built from **geometric primitives** — circles, squares, rectangles, and triangles — rather than drawn freehand. This produces icons that are mathematically precise, visually consistent, and easy to modify.

**The Icon Grid**
Before drawing any icon, establish a grid. The standard icon grid consists of:
- A **bounding box** — the outer boundary of the icon (e.g., 24×24 px)
- A **live area** — the safe zone within the bounding box where the icon content sits (e.g., 20×20 px, leaving 2 px padding on each side)
- **Key shapes** — a circle, square, and rectangle inscribed within the live area, used as guides for consistent sizing

All icons in a set should use the same grid. This ensures that icons appear the same visual weight when placed side by side, even if their shapes differ.

**Construction Process:**

**Step 1 — Identify the Core Shape**
Every icon has one primary shape that carries its meaning. For a home icon, it is a pentagon (house silhouette). For a settings icon, it is a circle (gear). For a document icon, it is a rectangle with a folded corner. Identify this shape first.

**Step 2 — Build from Primitives**
Construct the icon using only circles, rectangles, and triangles. Use the Pathfinder panel (in Illustrator) to unite, subtract, and intersect shapes — do not draw complex paths freehand.

**Step 3 — Apply Consistent Stroke Weight**
If the icon style is outlined (not filled), apply a consistent stroke weight across all icons in the set. A common standard is 2 px stroke at 24×24 px. Scale the stroke proportionally if the icon size changes.

**Step 4 — Apply Corner Radius**
Decide whether the icon set uses sharp corners or rounded corners — and apply this consistently. A corner radius of 2 px at 24×24 px produces a subtly friendly feel without being overly soft.

**Step 5 — Test at Target Size**
Zoom out to the actual display size and evaluate legibility. If any detail disappears or becomes ambiguous, simplify.

---

### 3.5 Icon Styles

| Style | Description | Best For |
|---|---|---|
| **Outline (Line)** | Icons drawn as strokes only — no fill inside the shapes | Interfaces, infographics, clean modern layouts |
| **Filled (Solid)** | Icons drawn as solid filled shapes — no visible stroke | High-contrast situations, small sizes, bold visual weight |
| **Duotone** | Two-color icons — typically a filled base shape with a lighter accent | Infographics, presentations, branded icon sets |
| **Flat** | Simplified, filled shapes with no gradients, shadows, or 3D effects | Modern interfaces, infographics, print |
| **Glyph** | Simplified silhouette — maximum contrast, minimum detail | Signage, wayfinding, accessibility-critical contexts |

> **Design Rule:** Choose one style and apply it consistently across the entire icon set. Mixing outline icons with filled icons in the same set creates visual inconsistency that undermines the professionalism of the design.

---

### 3.6 Icons in Infographics

In infographics, icons serve three specific roles:

**1. Category Markers** — Icons identify sections or categories, replacing or supplementing text labels. A person icon for "population," a factory icon for "industry," a leaf icon for "environment." This allows the audience to navigate the infographic without reading every label.

**2. Pictographs** — Icons represent quantities. In a pictograph, each icon represents a fixed number of units (e.g., each person icon = 1 million people). The audience counts icons to read the data. Pictographs are more engaging than bar charts for general audiences but less precise.

**3. Visual Anchors** — Large icons at the top of a section establish the topic visually before the data is presented. They give the eye a landing point and help the audience orient themselves within a complex layout.

**Rules for Icons in Infographics:**
- All icons in an infographic must come from the same set (same style, same stroke weight)
- Icons should be the same size within a section — size variation implies hierarchy or quantity
- Never use icons as pure decoration — every icon should label, quantify, or anchor a specific piece of information
- Pair icons with text labels in any context where the icon's meaning might be ambiguous

## PART 4: DESIGNING AN INFOGRAPHIC — PROCESS AND LAYOUT (20 minutes)

### 4.1 The Infographic Design Process

**Step 1 — Define the Message**
Before touching any design tool, answer one question: *What is the single most important thing the audience should understand after seeing this infographic?* Write it as a one-sentence headline. Every design decision that follows should serve this headline.

**Step 2 — Audit the Data**
Collect all data points. Identify:
- Which data points directly support the headline message
- Which data points provide useful context
- Which data points are interesting but not essential
- Which data points are irrelevant or potentially misleading

Cut ruthlessly. An infographic that tries to show everything shows nothing clearly.

**Step 3 — Choose the Structure**
Select the infographic type (statistical, informational, timeline, process, geographic, comparison) based on the nature of the data and the message. Sketch the overall layout on paper before opening any software.

**Step 4 — Select Chart Types**
For each data set, choose the chart type that most accurately represents the relationship in the data (see Part 2.2). Sketch rough versions of each chart.

**Step 5 — Design the Visual System**
Establish before building:
- **Color palette** — 1 primary accent color + 2–3 neutral tones
- **Typography** — 1 typeface family with 2–3 weight variations (bold for headlines, regular for labels, light for captions)
- **Icon style** — outline or filled; corner radius; stroke weight
- **Grid** — number of columns; gutter width; margin

**Step 6 — Build and Refine**
Build the infographic in vector software (Adobe Illustrator or equivalent). Apply the visual system consistently. After the first draft, evaluate:
- Does the headline insight read immediately?
- Is the visual hierarchy clear — does the eye move in the intended sequence?
- Is every element earning its place, or is there chartjunk to remove?
- Are all data representations accurate?

**Step 7 — Export**
- **PDF** — for print and high-quality digital distribution
- **PNG** — for web, social media, and presentations (minimum 1200 px wide for readability)
- **SVG** — for web embedding with full scalability

---

### 4.2 Common Infographic Layout Patterns

| Pattern | Description | Best For |
|---|---|---|
| **Top-to-bottom narrative** | Single column, reads like a story from headline to conclusion | Social media, long-form editorial |
| **Dashboard** | Multiple charts arranged in a grid — no single narrative flow | Data-heavy reports, business dashboards |
| **Timeline** | Horizontal or vertical axis with events plotted along it | Historical data, project timelines, process flows |
| **Comparison columns** | Two or three vertical columns, each representing one option or category | Product comparisons, before/after, pros/cons |
| **Map-centered** | A geographic map as the primary visual, with data overlaid | Location-based data, regional statistics |

---

### 4.3 Infographic Checklist

Before finalizing any infographic, verify:

**Accuracy**
- [ ] All data is correctly represented — no truncated axes, no misleading scales
- [ ] Data source is credited at the bottom
- [ ] All numbers add up correctly (pie chart = 100%, totals match)

**Hierarchy**
- [ ] The headline insight is the most visually prominent element
- [ ] Supporting data is clearly subordinate to the headline
- [ ] Labels and captions are the smallest text elements

**Simplicity**
- [ ] No chartjunk — every element encodes information
- [ ] Color is used purposefully — not decoratively
- [ ] Icons are consistent in style and size

**Accessibility**
- [ ] Color is not the only way to distinguish data (labels, patterns, or position also used)
- [ ] Text is large enough to read at the intended display size
- [ ] Sufficient contrast between text and background

## PART 5: GROUP ACTIVITY — BUILD A MINI INFOGRAPHIC (15 minutes)

### Activity Overview

Students are divided into **3 groups**. Each group receives a dataset and builds a mini infographic on paper (or digitally if time allows), then presents it to the class.

**Time:** 10 minutes design + 5 minutes presentations (approximately 90 seconds each)

---

### Group 1 — Statistical Infographic

**Dataset:** Social media usage among Filipino youth (ages 15–24):
- Facebook: 78%
- TikTok: 65%
- Instagram: 54%
- YouTube: 89%
- Twitter/X: 31%

**Tasks:**
1. Choose the correct chart type for this data and justify your choice.
2. Identify the headline insight (the single most important finding).
3. Sketch the layout: title, chart, labels, source line.
4. Apply a color rule: use one accent color for the highest value; grey for all others.
5. Present: *"We chose a ___ chart because ___. The headline insight is ___.\"*

---

### Group 2 — Process Infographic

**Dataset:** The 5 steps of the design process:
1. Research — understand the problem and the audience
2. Ideate — generate multiple concepts through sketching
3. Design — build the chosen concept in software
4. Review — evaluate against the brief; revise
5. Deliver — export in correct formats for the client

**Tasks:**
1. Choose a layout structure for a process infographic (numbered steps, flowchart, circular cycle).
2. Design a simple icon for each step using only basic shapes (circle, rectangle, triangle, line).
3. Write a one-sentence description for each step.
4. Sketch the complete layout with title, steps, icons, and descriptions.
5. Present: *"We used a ___ layout because ___. The icon for 'Research' is ___ because ___.\"*

---

### Group 3 — Comparison Infographic

**Dataset:** Raster vs. Vector graphics (key differences):

| | Raster | Vector |
|---|---|---|
| Made of | Pixels | Mathematical paths |
| Scalability | Loses quality when enlarged | Scales without quality loss |
| File size | Large at high resolution | Typically small |
| Best for | Photography, web images | Logos, icons, illustrations |
| Common formats | JPG, PNG, PSD | SVG, AI, EPS |

**Tasks:**
1. Design a two-column comparison layout with a clear visual divider.
2. Choose one icon to represent "Raster" and one to represent "Vector" — sketch both using geometric shapes.
3. Apply a color system: one color for Raster, one contrasting color for Vector, neutral for shared elements.
4. Write a one-sentence headline that states the most important difference.
5. Present: *"Our headline is ___. We used ___ color for Raster and ___ for Vector because ___.\"*

## PART 6: SUMMARY AND ASSESSMENT (10 minutes)

### 6.1 Key Takeaways

1. An **infographic** is a visual translation of information — its purpose is to make complex data faster to understand and easier to remember *(Mrantinkovic et al., 2020)*.
2. The six infographic types — **statistical, informational, timeline, process, geographic, and comparison** — each suit a different kind of data and message. Choosing the wrong type distorts the message.
3. Effective data visualization follows four principles: **accuracy, hierarchy, simplicity, and context**. Violating accuracy is not a design choice — it is a misrepresentation of data.
4. **Color** in data visualization encodes meaning — categorical, sequential, or diverging. It should never be used purely decoratively.
5. **Typography** in infographics follows a strict hierarchy: title → subtitle/annotation → axis labels → data labels → source. Each level should be visually distinct.
6. An **icon** is the most compressed form of visual communication — a single shape that must be immediately understood without text support *(Mrantinkovic et al., 2020)*.
7. Effective icons are **clear at small sizes, consistent within a system, and as simple as possible**. They are built from geometric primitives on a consistent grid.
8. Icons in infographics serve as **category markers, pictographs, or visual anchors** — never as pure decoration.
9. The infographic design process begins with a **single headline message** — every design decision must serve that message.

---

### 6.2 Assessment Questions

**Short Answer (Individual — 10 minutes):**

1. Define infographic in your own words. How does it differ from a plain data visualization?
2. A researcher has data showing the percentage of students who prefer each of five study methods. Which chart type would you recommend and why? Which chart type would be inappropriate and why?
3. Explain the four principles of data visualization. Give one example of how violating each principle would mislead the audience.
4. What is an icon system? List four visual properties that must be consistent across all icons in a system.
5. You are designing an infographic about climate change for a general audience. Describe your process: how would you define the headline message, choose the infographic type, select a color palette, and decide which icons to use?

### 6.3 Comparison Table: Effective vs. Ineffective Infographic Design

| Feature | Effective Infographic | Ineffective Infographic |
|---|---|---|
| **Headline** | States the key insight clearly and prominently | Missing, vague, or buried in the layout |
| **Chart type** | Matches the nature of the data and the message | Wrong type chosen — distorts or obscures the data |
| **Color** | Used purposefully to encode meaning and guide hierarchy | Used decoratively — rainbow palettes, random color assignment |
| **Typography** | Clear hierarchy — title, labels, captions are visually distinct | All text the same size and weight — no hierarchy |
| **Icons** | Consistent style, size, and stroke weight throughout | Mixed styles — some outlined, some filled, different weights |
| **Data accuracy** | All representations are mathematically honest | Truncated axes, misleading scales, segments that don't add to 100% |
| **Simplicity** | No chartjunk — every element encodes information | Decorative backgrounds, 3D effects, unnecessary gridlines |
| **Source** | Data source credited at the bottom | No source — data cannot be verified |

### 6.4 Assignment

**Design an Infographic and Icon Set**

Choose one of the following topics and design a complete infographic with a matching icon set:

- **Topic A:** The graphic design industry in the Philippines — employment statistics, top industries hiring designers, average salary ranges, and most in-demand skills.
- **Topic B:** The environmental impact of digital vs. print media — energy consumption, carbon footprint, paper usage, and e-waste statistics.
- **Topic C:** A step-by-step visual guide to the design process — from brief to delivery — aimed at a client who has never worked with a designer before.

**Deliverables:**

1. **Infographic (digital, A4 or vertical format)** — Built in Adobe Illustrator or equivalent vector software. Must include:
   - A clear headline stating the key insight
   - At least two different chart or data visualization types
   - A consistent color palette (maximum 3 colors + neutrals)
   - A minimum of 4 icons from a consistent icon set
   - Data source credited at the bottom

2. **Icon Set (minimum 6 icons)** — All icons must:
   - Be built on a consistent 24×24 px grid
   - Use the same stroke weight and corner radius
   - Be the same style (all outline or all filled)
   - Be exported as SVG files

3. **Written Reflection (5–7 sentences):** Identify the infographic type, explain the chart type choices, describe the color encoding strategy, and confirm that all icons follow a consistent visual system.

**Submission:** Next class meeting

**Evaluation Criteria:**

| Criterion | Description |
|---|---|
| Message clarity | The headline insight is immediately clear; the infographic tells a coherent visual story |
| Data accuracy | All charts and data representations are mathematically correct and sourced |
| Visual hierarchy | The eye moves through the infographic in a logical sequence from headline to detail |
| Icon consistency | All icons share the same grid, stroke weight, corner radius, and style |
| Design quality | Color, typography, and layout follow the principles covered in this module |
| Technical execution | Infographic is built in vector software; icons are exported as SVG |
